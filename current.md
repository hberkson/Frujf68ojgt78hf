6. ADD APPENDIX A — VULVA.

A1 Orientation: The vulva is upright. Hood and clitoris at the top of the frame, fourchette at the bottom, the seam running vertically between the closed lips, mons above the hood. If the seam runs across the frame, or the hood sits below the fourchette, it is rotated and wrong. One axis, one way to fail it.

A2 Spread and degradation: Failure order is fixed and monotonic — seam opens top-down; keyhole at the top of the seam; one lip thins to a pale ribbon; lips drop outward and down like moth wings; interior corrugates edge to edge; an invented second hole below the canal. It never recovers inside a clip — once a stage is reached the clip is failing, not recovering. NO RELEASE: fingers hold to the end; release is designed out because the return never happens.

A3 Tone and reference trade: Tone is an absolute plus a relationship. Absolute — interior must be pale; wording never lightens a reference; a bright frame is mandatory (the 7.7 tone rule, same rule). Relationship — every added reference buys one property and costs another, so the trade is logged: what was bought, what was paid. A third bright reference bought tone and cost labia length. Never add a reference without naming both sides.

A4 Edit-vs-grab: edit models redraw anatomy, they do not part it — never use an edit pass for a vulva end frame; grab from a run. Grabs from a good run beat stills.

7. ADD APPENDIX B — FLUID.

B1 Emission: origin is the slit at the tip; each pulse begins at the tip and travels away; tip lifted and held lifted throughout — always upward, never drops. No pre-emission bead, drip or ooze before the first pulse.

B2 Cadence: pulses at a stated interval, one every one to two seconds, each fired by a hand stroke. Air between them is mandatory. The count is fixed and small.

B3 Angle within an upward cone (read with B8): all pulses live inside a narrow cone angled upward from the tip, about thirty degrees wide, centred on her face. Inside it each pulse takes its own line — one flat, one steeper, one across. The cone keeps the flight upward; variation stops them reading as clones.

B4 Range: short. Tip three to six inches from target; the cord covers it fast. Long range loses force and turns to fall.

B5 Cap: no single landed mass wider than the pad of a thumb across; four masses maximum on the face; total coverage under a third. Anything wider than a thumb pad on one hit is the superhuman line — width per hit, not total.

B6 Adhesion and separation: viscosity and surface tension hold the mass — it coheres because it is thick. It separates where mass is least and acceleration greatest: behind the tip, where the stroke flings it, and along the thin tail. It does not break on contact; contact is where it stops, not where it comes apart.

B7 Visible consequence — the thread test: GOOD — cord thickens toward her and thins behind; the thin part stays caught on the skin, sagging, attached at both ends; at the break a visible thread runs from her back toward the tip and is always thinner than the landed mass. BAD — uniform width like a rope; a strand left hanging off the glans; a tail that snaps back or vanishes. The test is the thread: if nothing connects her to the tip for a beat after the break, the tail failed. The break is BEHIND the tip, never at it — origin is not a break point; a brief stub remains at the tip and is gone by the next stroke. Stub and thread are one event seen from both ends.

B8 Camera and the arc (read with B3): the camera sits ACROSS the arc, never along it — the flight is perpendicular to the lens. Lift is physical, not apparent: the tip is lifted because a down tip drips, not because it photographs better. Flat-from-above is NOT a fault — a near-horizontal flight seen from above is correct and reads as force. Only a downward flight is a fault.

B9 Translucency — PROVISIONAL: off-white, faintly warm; translucency exists only at the thin edge of a tail. If a run reads translucent through the body, the wording made it too thin, not too pale — fix weight, not colour. Provisional: named in the wording; the first keeper run decides whether the mark stays or the line is rewritten.
